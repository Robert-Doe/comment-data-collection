1 What cross-validation actually does

Here is the single most important thing to internalize before anything else:

What is cross-validation for?

Cross-validation is a measurement tool, not a training tool. It answers the question: "If I train a model using this algorithm and this imbalance strategy, how good would it be — reliably, not just on a lucky split?" It does not produce the model you deploy. It tells you whether it is safe to train the model you will deploy.

Think of it like a clinical trial. You run 5 independent trials of the same treatment on 5 different patient cohorts. You are not choosing which cohort to keep — you are measuring whether the treatment reliably works. Once you have that confidence, you administer the treatment to everyone.

The analogy that sticks: CV is like checking the weather from 5 different forecasting services before deciding whether to plan an outdoor event. You are not picking one forecast to deploy — you are using all 5 to build confidence in what the weather will actually be.

2 The 5-fold loop, step by step

In this system, the 5 folds are based on domain buckets — every domain is deterministically assigned to one of 5 buckets using hash(hostname) % 5. This means the same domain always lands in the same bucket, and all rows from a domain stay together (no leakage from the same site appearing in both train and test).

For each fold, the system:

  1. Trains a fresh model on the 4 training buckets
  2. Scores every row in the held-out test bucket
  3. Computes F1, Precision, Recall, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC at the 0.50 threshold
  4. Also sweeps thresholds to find the one that maximises F1 for that fold
  5. Records calibration (predicted probability vs. actual positive rate)
  6. Then discards the model weights
Step 6 surprises people every time: The fold model is not saved. Its weights are thrown away after measuring performance. Only the metrics survive. This is intentional — fold models are probes, not products.

? Which of the 5 models gets used?

The answer you came here for

None of them. All 5 fold-models are discarded. Zero. They are measurement instruments, not the final product. After cross-validation, you train a completely separate, 6th model on all of your labeled data using the algorithm and strategy that CV told you was reliable. That 6th model is the one you deploy.

Why not just keep the best-performing fold model? Three reasons:

Problem 1 — It trained on less data

  • Every fold model only saw 80% of your labeled data
  • The final model trained on 100% will almost always be stronger
  • You have the data — use all of it

Problem 2 — Its test set is no longer unseen

  • Once you pick a fold model, its "test" bucket has already influenced your decision
  • That leaks information and inflates your confidence in the threshold
  • The fairness guarantee breaks down

Problem 3 — Selection bias

  • If you pick the best-fold model, you are capitalising on luck
  • The fold that happened to get easier domains will look best
  • That bias will not generalise to production traffic

The right path

  • Use CV to validate the configuration (algorithm + strategy)
  • Once satisfied, click Train in Model Lab — same settings, all data
  • That model gets the threshold curve and gets deployed

4 What the CV statistics are actually telling you

After 5 folds, you have 5 F1 scores, 5 thresholds, 5 ROC-AUC values, and so on. The system computes mean ± std and a 95% confidence interval across those 5 values. Here is how to read them:

What you see What it means in plain English Good signal?
F1 mean = 0.81 On average, across all 5 test windows, the model gets an F1 of 0.81 Strong
F1 std = 0.02 The folds agreed closely — low sensitivity to which domains ended up as test Stable
F1 std = 0.15 The folds disagreed a lot — one domain bucket is much harder or easier than the others Unstable
95% CI [0.77–0.85] If you trained 100 times on random splits, F1 would land here 95% of the time Tight = confident
Best F1 ★ mean = 0.86 Average F1 when each fold used its own optimal threshold (not fixed 0.50) Shows threshold potential
Best threshold = 0.34 On average, folds needed to lower the threshold below 0.50 to hit their best F1 Informs where to start for final model
The gap between F1 @0.50 and Best F1 ★ tells you something important: A large gap (e.g. 0.72 vs. 0.86) means the model's raw probabilities are shifted — it is systematically under-confident about positives. The threshold for the final model will likely need to be below 0.50. A small gap means 0.50 is already near-optimal.

Also notice: the average best threshold across all folds is about 0.31, not 0.50. This is a signal you will use when picking the final model's threshold.

5 Training the final model on all your data

Once CV has convinced you the configuration works, you click Train in Model Lab (same variant, same algorithm, same imbalance strategy). This time, there is no fold rotation — the full labeled dataset is used for training, and the model is saved to disk as a real artifact.

📋

All labeled data → Training set

Every row that has a label goes in. No bucket withheld. This is more data than any single fold had, so the final model is typically stronger than any fold model was.

⚙️

Same algorithm + imbalance strategy as CV

You use exactly what CV validated. Switching the algorithm at this point would mean CV told you nothing useful.

📊

Model scores itself on the training data

After fitting, the model scores every training row to produce a distribution of predicted probabilities. This is used to sweep the threshold curve.

💾

Model artifact saved to disk

Weights, vectorizer, threshold curve, and evaluation metrics are all written to the artifact directory. This is the model that production will load.

Why does the final model train on all data if CV held some back? CV proved your approach is sound. Now you want the strongest possible model — and more training data is almost always better. The CV validation already gave you the honest estimate of how it will perform on unseen domains. The final model is just putting that approach to full use.

6 How the final threshold is chosen

The final model outputs a probability between 0 and 1 for each candidate (higher = more likely spam). The threshold is the cutoff above which you call something positive. You need to choose it.

After training, the system runs a threshold curve: it tries every threshold from 0.05 to 0.95 in small steps and records the resulting Precision, Recall, and F1 at each step. Then it picks the threshold that gives the highest F1.

Notice what happens as you drag the slider:

  • Too low a threshold (e.g. 0.10) — nearly everything gets flagged as spam. Recall is perfect but Precision collapses. Lots of false positives.
  • Too high a threshold (e.g. 0.85) — only the most obvious spam gets caught. Precision is perfect but Recall collapses. Lots of missed spam.
  • The sweet spot — somewhere in the middle, F1 peaks. That is where the system lands.
What about the per-fold thresholds from CV? The average best threshold from CV (e.g. 0.31 in our example) is useful as a sanity check — if the final model's optimal threshold lands near that range, everything is consistent. But the final threshold is determined independently from the final model's own scoring run, not averaged from the folds. The fold thresholds are hints, not inputs.

The three-way trade-off you are always navigating

Lower threshold → higher Recall

  • Catches more spam — fewer misses
  • Also catches more legitimate comments (false positives)
  • Right choice when missing spam is costly

Higher threshold → higher Precision

  • Everything flagged is almost certainly spam
  • Misses some real spam (false negatives)
  • Right choice when false positives are costly

F1 maximisation — the default

  • F1 is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall — it forces a balance
  • Picking the threshold that maximises F1 is the standard starting point
  • You can adjust it afterward based on production feedback (e.g. "we're seeing too many false positives" → raise threshold by 0.05)

7 When to proceed vs. when to re-tune

After CV, you have to make a judgment call. Here is a practical rubric:

SignalMeaningAction
F1 std < 0.05 Folds agree closely — the model is stable across domain distributions Proceed to train final model
F1 std > 0.10 One or more folds are wildly different — some domains are far harder or easier Inspect domain buckets; consider adding more data from the weak bucket
One fold is much worse That bucket likely contains a domain cluster with unusual patterns Use "Inspect Domain Buckets" to see which domains are in the problem fold
Best F1 ★ much higher than F1 @0.50 The model is systematically under-confident — probabilities are compressed toward the middle Expected and normal; the threshold curve will correct for this
ROC-AUC > 0.85 but F1 is low The model ranks correctly but needs threshold tuning — class imbalance is likely the cause Try SMOTE or class weight strategies, or rely on threshold tuning
ROC-AUC < 0.75 across all folds The model is not separating spam from ham reliably Re-examine features; add data; try a different algorithm
Some folds skipped A bucket had fewer than the minimum samples needed to train Collect more labeled data or relax the minimum; check the skip reason

8 The complete workflow, start to finish

What a normal day looks like in Model Lab

  1. Pick a variant, algorithm, and imbalance strategy in the Diagnostics panel.
  2. Run 5-Fold Cross-Validation. Wait 1–5 minutes. Review the aggregate table.
  3. Check the std. Is it below 0.05? Good. Is one fold wildly off? Use "Inspect Domain Buckets" to understand why.
  4. Note the average best threshold from the fold results. It tells you roughly where the final model's threshold will land (useful as a sanity check later).
  5. Optionally run the Learning Curve to check whether adding more training data would still help (gap between train and test F1 closing = saturation reached).
  6. If CV looks good, go to Train Variants and click Train with the same settings. This trains on all data — not a fold.
  7. The trained model artifact appears in Trained Models. Its evaluation card shows the threshold curve result — the threshold that maximised F1 on its own scoring run.
  8. Verify the final threshold makes sense. It should be near the average fold threshold from step 4. If it is very different, investigate why (data distribution shift, imbalance correction side-effect, etc.).
  9. Deploy the model by setting it as active. The threshold from the artifact is what the live scanner uses.
The three-sentence version: Cross-validation runs 5 temporary models to measure how reliable your configuration is — none of those models survive. Once you trust the configuration, you train a real model on all your data and that model picks its own optimal threshold from a sweep of its own probability scores. The fold thresholds are reference points, not the answer.
